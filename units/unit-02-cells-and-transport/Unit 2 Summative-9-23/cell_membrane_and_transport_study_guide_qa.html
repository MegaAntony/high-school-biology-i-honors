Question 1

What is a phospholipid? Draw and label one (hydrophobic, hydrophilic, polar, non-polar parts).

Answer: A phospholipid is an amphipathic lipid molecule that forms the primary structural matrix of all biological membranes.

  • Phosphate Head: Polar and hydrophilic ("water-loving"). Faces the aqueous fluid inside and outside the cell.
  • Two Fatty Acid Tails: Non-polar and hydrophobic ("water-fearing"). Face inward, shielded from water. One tail is saturated (straight), and one is unsaturated (contains a cis-double bond that creates a kink, preventing tight packing and maintaining membrane fluidity).

Question 2

What three components are phospholipids made of?

  1. Glycerol Backbone: A 3-carbon alcohol molecule that anchors the head and tails together.
  2. Phosphate Group: Negatively charged hydrophilic group attached to carbon-3 of the glycerol (often bonded to a small organic molecule such as choline).
  3. Two Fatty Acid Chains: Long non-polar hydrocarbon tails attached to carbons 1 and 2 of the glycerol.

Question 4

What does it mean to be selectively permeable? HOW does the phospholipid bilayer accomplish this?

Definition: Selective permeability means the membrane regulates traffic, allowing some substances to cross freely while blocking others or requiring specialized transport proteins.

How it is accomplished: The hydrophobic interior of fatty acid tails acts as a physical and chemical barrier:

  • Freely Permeable: Small, non-polar molecules ($\text{O}_2, \text{CO}_2, \text{N}_2$, steroids) dissolve directly in the lipid core and diffuse across without assistance.
  • Impermeable (Blocked): Ions ($\text{Na}^+, \text{K}^+, \text{Ca}^{2+}, \text{Cl}^-$) are repelled by the hydrophobic core. Large polar molecules (glucose, sucrose, amino acids) are too bulky and polar to cross unaided, requiring channel or carrier proteins.
Questions 5–8

Passive Transport Types & Scientific Drawings (Diffusion, Facilitated Diffusion, Osmosis)

Rule: Passive transport requires ZERO metabolic energy ($0\text{ ATP}$) and moves solutes DOWN their concentration gradient ($\text{HIGH} \to \text{LOW}$).
Q5 & Q6

Simple Diffusion

Net movement of small, nonpolar solutes directly through bilayer from High to Low concentration.

Example: $\text{O}_2\text{ into blood}$
Q5 & Q7

Facilitated Diffusion

Passive transport through transmembrane proteins for polar molecules and charged ions.

Channel: Fixed open pore (aquaporins).
Carrier: Changes shape (GLUT glucose).
Q5 & Q8

Osmosis

Net diffusion of free water across a membrane from low solute to high solute.

Moves to dilute the solute!
Questions 9–13

Osmotic Solutions, Cell Drawings & Plant vs. Animal Responses (☺ ☹ :/)

Q9 & Q10

Hypotonic

Solute Out < In

Water rushes INTO the cell.

Why? Plant cell wall prevents bursting; creates high turgor pressure.

Q9 & Q12

Isotonic

Solute Out = In

No net movement (Dynamic Equilibrium).

Why? Animal cells thrive in 0.9% saline; plants droop/wilt without turgor.

Q9 & Q11

Hypertonic

Solute Out > In

Water rushes OUT of the cell.

Why? Membrane pulls away from cell wall; cell shrivels.

Questions 14–17

Active Transport Mechanisms, Bulk Transport & Comparison

Rule: Active transport requires metabolic energy ($\text{ATP}$) and moves solutes AGAINST their gradient ($\text{LOW} \to \text{HIGH}$).
Q14

The 3 Types of Active Transport

  1. Molecular Pumps: Transmembrane proteins hydrolyzing ATP directly to move ions against concentration gradients.
    Flagship: Sodium-Potassium ($\text{Na}^+/\text{K}^+$) Pump: Exports $3\text{ Na}^+\text{ OUT}$ and imports $2\text{ K}^+\text{ IN}$ using $1\text{ ATP}$.
  2. Endocytosis (Bulk Import - Q15): Plasma membrane folds inward to form vesicles enclosing extracellular cargo.
  3. Exocytosis (Bulk Export - Q16): Vesicles from the Golgi fuse with the plasma membrane, releasing hormones/waste outside.
Q15

The 3 Types of Endocytosis

  • Phagocytosis ("Cell Eating"): Membrane engulfs large, solid particles or whole bacteria into a phagosome for lysosomal digestion.
  • Pinocytosis ("Cell Drinking"): Membrane invaginates to take in droplets of extracellular fluid containing dissolved solutes non-specifically.
  • Receptor-Mediated Endocytosis: Extremely specific. Cell-surface receptor proteins bind specific target molecules (e.g., LDL cholesterol), triggering coated vesicle formation.
Question 17: What is the main difference between passive and active transport?
Passive Transport: • Requires $0\text{ ATP}$.
• Moves solutes DOWN gradient ($\text{High} \to \text{Low}$).
Active Transport: • Requires cellular $\text{ATP}$ energy.
• Moves solutes AGAINST gradient ($\text{Low} \to \text{High}$).
