## 2. Pages 3–4: Cell Membrane Anatomy, Flexibility & TRACIE Functions

### Structural Anatomy of the Plasma Membrane
- **Extracellular Space:** Aqueous fluid surrounding the exterior of the cell.
- **Intracellular Space (Cytoplasm):** Aqueous fluid within the cell containing organelles.
- **Why is the membrane flexible?**
  - The phospholipids are not covalently bonded together. They are held in a double layer solely by weak hydrophobic interactions between their fatty acid tails.
  - Phospholipids and unanchored proteins vibrate, rotate, and diffuse laterally within the layer, creating a fluid consistency comparable to olive oil.
- **Why is it called a Fluid Mosaic Model?**
  - **"Fluid":** Individual phospholipids and proteins move laterally within the bilayer.
  - **"Mosaic":** The surface is studded with an intricate pattern of diverse biomolecules: integral proteins, peripheral proteins, cholesterol, glycoproteins, and glycolipids.

### Membrane Components
- **Glycoprotein:** Transmembrane or surface protein covalently bonded to an oligosaccharide carbohydrate chain. Functions in cell-cell recognition and immune signaling.
- **Glycolipid:** Membrane lipid covalently bonded to a carbohydrate chain. Promotes membrane stability and cellular identification.
- **Integral Membrane Protein:** Protein that permanently spans or penetrates the hydrophobic core of the bilayer.
- **Peripheral Membrane Protein:** Protein loosely attached to the inner or outer leaflet of the membrane or bound to integral proteins.
- **Cholesterol:** 4-ring steroid lipid interspersed among hydrophobic tails that modulates membrane fluidity.
- **Cytoskeleton Filaments:** Microfilaments anchored to internal peripheral proteins to give structural support.

### Membrane Jobs (TRACIE Mnemonic)
- **T — Transport:** Protein channels and carrier pumps moving molecules across the membrane.
- **R — Receptors:** Peptide hormones and signal ligands binding to surface proteins to trigger signal transduction.
- **A — Anchorage:** Cytoskeletal microfilaments anchoring internally; extracellular matrix anchoring externally.
- **C — Cell Recognition:** Oligosaccharide tags identifying cell type, tissue origin, and self vs. non-self.
- **I — Intercellular Joinings:** Tight junctions, gap junctions, and desmosomes hooking cells together.
- **E — Enzymatic Activity:** Membrane-localized enzymes catalyzing regional biochemical pathways.

## 3. Pages 5–8: Lecture Guided Notes (Diffusion, Osmosis, Facilitated & Active Transport)

### Page 5: Passive Transport & Simple Diffusion
- **Passive Cell Transport:** Movement of substances across a membrane without expenditure of metabolic cellular energy (0 ATP), moving **down** the concentration gradient (**High $\rightarrow$ Low**).
- **Simple Diffusion:** Spontaneous movement of solute particles through the phospholipid bilayer from high concentration to low concentration until dynamic equilibrium is reached.

### Page 6: Osmosis in Animal vs. Plant Cells
Osmosis is the simple diffusion of water molecules across a selectively permeable membrane toward higher solute concentration (lower water potential).

| Condition | Animal Cells (e.g., RBC) | Plant Cells (e.g., *Elodea*) |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Hypertonic Solution**<br>(High solute / Low water outside) | Water diffuses OUT.<br>Cell shrivels and shrinks (**Crenation**). | Water diffuses OUT.<br>Cytoplasm pulls away from cell wall (**Plasmolysis**); plant wilts. |
| **Isotonic Solution**<br>(Equal solute & water) | Water enters & exits at equal rates.<br>Normal volume (**Dynamic Equilibrium**). | Equal movement.<br>Cell is limp/flaccid; lacks turgor. |
| **Hypotonic Solution**<br>(Low solute / High water outside) | Water diffuses IN.<br>Cell swells and bursts (**Lysis / Cytolysis**). | Water diffuses IN.<br>Cell swells against rigid wall; becomes **Turgid** (ideal state). |

#### Practice — Osmosis Scenarios (Page 6)
1. **Beaker U-Tube Setup:**
   - Membrane permeable to water only. Side A has higher sucrose concentration; Side B has lower sucrose concentration.
   - *Which side has the highest water potential?* **Side B** (lower solute means higher free water).
   - *Which side has the highest solute concentration?* **Side A**.
   - *In which direction will water move?* **From Side B $\rightarrow$ Side A** (toward higher solute).
2. **Cell in Glucose Solution:**
   - Cell interior: $90\%$ water ($10\%$ glucose).
   - Surrounding solution: $10\%$ water ($90\%$ glucose).
   - *What type of solution surrounds the cell?* **Hypertonic solution** ($90\%$ solute outside vs. $10\%$ inside).
   - *Where will the water move?* Water diffuses **OUT of the cell** (from $90\%$ water inside to $10\%$ water outside).
   - *What will happen to the cell?* The cell will **shrivel and shrink** (plasmolysis/crenation).

### Page 7: Facilitated Diffusion & Active Transport Overview
- **Facilitated Diffusion:** Passive transport of large, polar, or charged molecules across the membrane down their concentration gradient using channel or carrier proteins.
  - *How does glucose enter the cell?* Via **facilitated diffusion** through specific **carrier or channel proteins (GLUT)** because glucose is too large and polar to pass directly through the hydrophobic lipid tails.
- **Active Cell Transport:** Movement of molecules **against** their concentration gradient (**Low $\rightarrow$ High**) requiring metabolic energy (**ATP**).
  - *Exocytosis:* Bulk export of substances via intracellular vesicles fusing with the plasma membrane.
  - *Endocytosis:* Bulk import of materials by plasma membrane invaginating to pinch off an intracellular vesicle.

---

### Page 8: Types of Endocytosis & The Sodium-Potassium Pump
- **Three Types of Endocytosis:**
  1. **Phagocytosis ("Cell Eating"):** Engulfing large solid particles (bacteria, debris) using pseudopodia to form a **phagosome**, which fuses with a lysosome for digestion.
  2. **Pinocytosis ("Cell Drinking"):** Non-specific invagination taking in small droplets of extracellular fluid and dissolved solutes.
  3. **Receptor-Mediated Endocytosis:** Specific extracellular ligands bind to outer membrane receptors clustered in clathrin-coated pits, triggering targeted vesicular internalization.
- **The Sodium-Potassium Pump ($Na^+/K^+$ ATPase):**
  - Step 1: 3 intracellular $Na^+$ ions bind to high-affinity sites on the transport protein.
  - Step 2: 1 ATP molecule is hydrolyzed; a phosphate group is transferred to the protein (phosphorylation).
  - Step 3: Protein undergoes a conformational shape change, expelling **3 $Na^+$ OUT** of the cell.
  - Step 4: 2 extracellular $K^+$ ions bind to the protein; phosphate is released (dephosphorylation).
  - Step 5: Protein snaps back to its original shape, importing **2 $K^+$ INTO** the cell.
  - *Charge Effect:* Net loss of 1 positive charge per cycle makes the inside of the cell slightly negative relative to the outside (resting membrane potential).

## 4. Page 9: Cell Membrane Reading Comprehension (Questions 1–8)

### Text Transcript
> *Phospholipids are lipids that have a polar head and nonpolar tail. The heads are hydrophilic (water loving) and the tails are chains of fatty acids.*  
> *The cell membrane is constantly vibrating, creating small openings within the structure. Therefore, the phospholipids can allow water and other smaller molecules to pass through into or out of the cell, without the use of energy. This type of passive transport is known as diffusion because the molecules are moving with the concentration gradient (high $\rightarrow$ low).*  
> *Another type of lipid in the cell membrane is cholesterol (D), which makes the membrane more fluid and adds to its flexibility. Embedded in the phospholipid bilayer are proteins that also aid in diffusion and cell recognition. Proteins called integral proteins (E) go all the way through the bilayer. Large molecules like glucose utilize these channel proteins to help move across cell membrane without the use of energy. Peripheral proteins (F) are a type of membrane protein that is attached to the surface of the biological membrane. Some membrane proteins, called glycoproteins (G), have carbohydrate chains (H) attached to help cells recognize each other and certain molecules.*

### Questions & Complete Model Answers

1. **What is another name for the cell membrane? Identify the overall structure of the cell membrane.**  
   - **Answer:** The plasma membrane. Its overall structure is a **phospholipid bilayer** with embedded proteins, cholesterol, and surface carbohydrates (described by the Fluid Mosaic Model).
2. **Define hydrophilic. Which portion of the bilayer is hydrophilic?**  
   - **Answer:** Hydrophilic means "water-loving" (polar or charged, readily interacting with water). The **phosphate heads** of the phospholipids facing the extracellular and intracellular environments are hydrophilic.
3. **Define hydrophobic. Which portion of the bilayer is hydrophobic?**  
   - **Answer:** Hydrophobic means "water-fearing" (nonpolar, insoluble in water). The interior fatty acid hydrocarbon tails of the phospholipids are hydrophobic.
4. **When does the movement of materials in and out of the cell NOT require energy?**  
   - **Answer:** When molecules move passively **down their concentration gradient** (from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration) via simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, or osmosis.
5. **List the responsibilities of the cell membrane.**  
   - **Answer:** Regulates selective transport of materials in and out of the cell, protects internal organelles, maintains cellular homeostasis, provides structural anchorage to cytoskeleton, and enables cell-to-cell communication and immune recognition.
6. **Describe the function of the proteins embedded in the cell membrane.**  
   - **Answer:** Integral channel and carrier proteins transport polar molecules and ions; receptor proteins transmit extracellular signals; peripheral proteins anchor cytoskeletal filaments and participate in enzymatic reactions.
7. **How are cells able to recognize various molecules and other types of cells?**  
   - **Answer:** Through **glycoproteins and glycolipids**—carbohydrate chains protruding from the outer membrane surface that act as unique biochemical fingerprints/markers.
8. **What component(s) of the cell membrane contribute to the fluid quality of the cell membrane?**  
   - **Answer:** The constant lateral motion and vibrations of the unsaturated fatty acid tails of phospholipids, along with interspersed **cholesterol** molecules that act as fluidity buffers.

### Part 1: Video Comprehension Questions & Answers
1. **Diffusion Labeling:**
   - Draw an arrow showing particles traveling from **High Concentration $\rightarrow$ Low Concentration** until dynamic equilibrium is achieved.

   - *Osmosis can be viewed as water moving to a **HIGHER** concentration of solute molecules.*
   - *Why can it also be viewed this way?* Because water diffuses toward hypertonic regions to hydrate and dissolve solute particles, effectively equalizing concentrations across the semipermeable barrier.
3. **Freshwater Fish in Saltwater Scenario:**
   - *What happens if a freshwater fish is placed into the ocean?*
   - Internal tissues of the fish are **hypotonic**; surrounding ocean water is **hypertonic**.
   - Water rapidly flows **OUT of the fish’s cells** via osmosis into the ocean. The fish suffers severe cellular dehydration, shrivels, and dies.
4. **Brain Swelling (Cerebral Edema) Clinical Treatment:**
   - *Should doctors administer a hypertonic or hypotonic IV saline solution to reduce brain swelling?*
   - **Answer: Hypertonic saline** (e.g., $3\%$ NaCl or mannitol).
   - *Explanation:* Introducing hypertonic saline into the bloodstream raises blood solute concentration above that of the brain tissue. By osmosis, excess fluid is drawn out of the swollen neurons and into the blood capillaries, relieving intracranial pressure.

### Part 2: The Gummy Bear Mystery
> *Scenario: Gummy bears (high in gelatin and sugar) are submerged in pure ice water for 30 minutes. Upon returning, they are massively swollen.*  
- **Correct Viewpoint:** **Friend Joe is correct.**
- **Scientific Rationale:** The gummy bear is strongly **hypertonic** (packed with sugar and gelatin solutes), while the ice water is **hypotonic** (virtually pure $H_2O$). Water diffuses by osmosis from the hypotonic water into the hypertonic gummy bear, causing it to swell. Sugar molecules cannot diffuse out because the gelatin matrix traps them.

### Part 3: Red Blood Cell Tonicity Identification
- **Swelling / Bursting Cells:** Placed in a **hypotonic solution** (cells are *hypertonic* compared to the solution).
- **Shriveled / Crenated Cells:** Placed in a **hypertonic solution** (cells are *hypotonic* compared to the solution).
- **Normal Disc Cells:** Placed in an **isotonic solution** (cells are *isotonic* with the solution).

## 8. Pages 15–17: Osmosis Gel Deco Cube / Gummy Bear Lab Investigation

### Background & Task
Vertebrate red blood cells (erythrocytes) are biconcave, flexible discs packed with hemoglobin without a nucleus or mitochondria. When placed in non-isotonic fluids, their shape and volume change drastically. In this lab, students use semipermeable **gel deco cubes** (or gummy bears) to model cellular osmotic responses across various external solutions.

### Pre-Lab Design Questions (Page 16)
1. **What is your independent variable (IV)?**  
   - The type/concentration of the surrounding soaking solution ($0\%$ distilled water, tap water, concentrated salt/sucrose solution).
2. **What is your dependent variable (DV)?**  
   - The change in mass (grams) and change in volume ($cm^3$) of the cube after incubation.
3. **Which group serves as your control in your experiment?**  
   - The cube placed in an isotonic solution (or initial pre-treatment baseline measurement).
4. **How will you ensure high-quality data and reduce experimental error?**  
   - Gently blot excess surface liquid with a paper towel before weighing, use the exact same electronic balance for all trials, measure dimensions with a metric ruler carefully, and run triplicate trials.
5. **What type of graph will you use to display your data?**  
   - A **bar graph** comparing the percent change in mass ($\%\ \Delta\text{Mass}$) across the different solution treatments.

### Data Collection Table

### Post-Lab Analysis Questions (Pages 16–17)
1. **What does the cube represent in this experiment?**  
   - A model cell enclosed by a selectively permeable membrane.
2. **What substance is actually passing through the membrane? Explain.**  
   - **Water ($H_2O$) only.** The large polymer chains of the gel matrix (or gelatin/sugar) are too massive to cross the membrane pores, so only small water molecules diffuse across.
3. **What does a positive change in mass or volume actually represent?**  
   - A net influx of water molecules into the cube via osmosis caused by a hypotonic external environment.
7. **Predict the change in mass of a cell placed in an isotonic environment. Explain.**  
   - The percent change in mass will be **$0\%$** because water diffuses into and out of the cell at identical, equal rates (dynamic equilibrium).

## 9. Pages 17–18: Clinical Case Study — Acute Water Intoxication CER

### Clinical Case Narrative
In 2007, 28-year-old Jennifer Strange participated in KDND 107.9 FM's radio contest *"Hold your wee for a Wii"* in Sacramento, California. Over several hours, contestants drank an 8-ounce bottle of water every 15 minutes without urinating. Jennifer consumed nearly 2 gallons of water. She developed a severe headache, dizziness, and vomiting. She went home and was found dead hours later from **acute water intoxication (hyponatremia)**.

### Scientific CER Argumentation

- **Driving Question:** *Can consuming too much water be deadly?*
- **Claim:**  
  Yes, drinking extreme quantities of water in a short time frame without urinating can be fatal because it causes acute hyponatremia and fatal osmotic brain swelling (cerebral edema).
- **Evidence:**  
  - Jennifer ingested nearly two gallons of pure water over three hours while retaining all urine.
  - Her blood sodium concentration plummeted far below normal physiological levels ($135 - 145\ \text{mEq/L}$).
  - She exhibited classic neuro-osmotic symptoms: excruciating headache, loss of motor balance, emesis, followed by coma and death.
  - The coroner's autopsy confirmed death secondary to massive cerebral edema and brainstem herniation.
- **Reasoning:**  
  When water consumption drastically exceeds the kidneys' maximum filtration rate (~$0.8 - 1.0\ \text{L/hour}$), blood plasma becomes profoundly **hypotonic** relative to intracellular fluids. By **osmosis**, water diffuses down its concentration gradient across the blood-brain barrier through neuronal **aquaporins** into brain cells. Unlike plant cells that have rigid cellulose cell walls to generate protective turgor pressure, animal cells lack cell walls and swell. Because the brain is enclosed inside the rigid, unyielding bone of the skull, swollen brain tissue builds fatal intracranial pressure, cutting off cerebral blood flow and herniating the brainstem downward, which arrests cardiac and respiratory centers.

*Context: Three identical houseplants are given different watering treatments:*  
- *Plant X is given normal tap water.*  
- *Plant Y is given heavily concentrated chemical fertilizer solution.*

   - **Answer:** Plant X maintained normal cellular homeostasis. Tap water is approximately isotonic/slightly hypotonic, allowing root cells to maintain healthy upright **turgor pressure**.

   - **Answer:** **Yes.** The concentrated chemical fertilizer created a strongly **hypertonic** soil solution. Water moved by osmosis **out** of the root cells and into the soil. 4. **Did plant Z's cells burst due to cytolysis? Explain.**  
   - **Answer:** **No.** Plant cells do not burst in hypotonic environments because their rigid **cellulose cell wall** exerts opposing wall pressure that balances incoming osmotic pressure, making the cells happily **turgid**.

    - **Answer:** **Side A is now at $7\text{ cm}$.**

Consequently, Side A loses water volume and its level falls to $7\text{ cm}$, while Side B gains volume and its level rises to $13\text{ cm}$.
