2. Page 3: History of Cell Discovery & The Cell Theory

Timeline of Historical Milestones

  • 1665 — Robert Hooke: Observed cork tissue with an early microscope; saw tiny empty compartments and coined the term "cells".
  • 1674+ — Anton van Leeuwenhoek: Improved lens magnification; first to observe living unicellular microbes ("animalcules") in pond water and saliva.
  • 1838 — Matthias Schleiden: Concluded that all plants are made of cells.
  • 1839 — Theodor Schwann: Concluded that all animals are made of cells (Schwann sounds like "swan").
  • 1855 — Rudolf Virchow: Stated that all cells come only from pre-existing cells via cell division ("Omnis cellula e cellula").

The 3 Core Tenets of Cell Theory

  1. All living things are made of cells.
  2. Cells are the basic unit of life.
  3. All cells come from other pre-existing cells.

3. Pages 4–5: Prokaryotes vs. Eukaryotes & Plant vs. Animal Cells

Universal Components of ALL Cells (Page 4)

Every single cell on Earth possesses:
1. Cell Membrane: Regulates selective transport and maintains homeostasis.
2. Cytoplasm: Jelly-like fluid suspending nutrients and internal structures.
3. Ribosomes: Non-membrane-bound molecular machines that synthesize proteins.
4. Genetic Material (DNA): Hereditary blueprints for cellular function.

4. Page 6: Viruses — Characteristics, Bacteriophage Structure & Replication

Are Viruses Living Organisms?

  • No. Viruses are classified as non-living infectious particles.
  • Evidence why viruses are non-living:
  • Acellular (not composed of cells; violate Cell Theory).
  • Incapable of autonomous reproduction; obligate intracellular parasites that require a host cell.
  • Possess no independent metabolism; cannot produce their own ATP.
  • Contain no ribosomes or protein synthesis machinery.

Features of All Viruses

  1. Capsid: Protective outer protein shell composed of capsomere subunits.
  2. Nucleic Acid Genome: Core containing either DNA or RNA (single- or double-stranded).
  3. Viral Envelope (in some viruses): Outer lipid bilayer membrane acquired from host plasma membrane.

Structure of a Bacteriophage (Virus that Infects Bacteria)

  • Capsid Head: 20-sided icosahedral protein head enclosing viral DNA.
  • Sheath: Contractile protein neck/shaft that acts as a syringe.
  • Base Plate: Anchoring platform with enzymatic pins to penetrate bacterial peptidoglycan.
  • Tail Fibers: Spider-like protein fibers that recognize and bind specific surface receptors on bacteria.

6. Page 9: Practice Questions on Organelles & Macromolecules

  1. What type of macromolecule is primarily found in cell walls?
    - Answer: Carbohydrates (specifically structural polysaccharides: cellulose in plants, chitin in fungi, and peptidoglycan in bacteria).
  2. Which organelle do you think would work closely with the rough ER? Why?
    - Answer: Ribosomes (which stud the ER surface to inject nascent polypeptide chains directly into its lumen) and the Golgi Apparatus (which receives transport vesicles budding from the rough ER to further process, glycosylate, and sort proteins).
  3. What organelle helps construct phospholipids needed by the cell membrane?
    - Answer: The Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum (Smooth ER) synthesizes fatty acids, phospholipids, and cholesterol.
  4. Which organelle(s) would generate many vesicles and why?
    - Answer: The Rough ER and Golgi Apparatus produce continuous streams of transport and secretory vesicles to shuttle proteins and lipids through the endomembrane pipeline; the Plasma Membrane generates pinocytic and phagocytic vesicles during endocytosis.

8. Pages 12–14: Organelle Hexagonal Systems Activity & Analysis Questions

Hexagonal Matrix System Connections (Page 12)

Cells do not function as isolated parts; they operate as integrated organelle systems:
1. The Protein Synthesis & Secretion Pipeline:
$$ ext{Nucleus (transcription)} \longrightarrow ext{Ribosomes (translation)} \longrightarrow ext{Rough ER (folding)} \longrightarrow ext{Transport Vesicle} \longrightarrow ext{Golgi (sorting)} \longrightarrow ext{Secretory Vesicle} \longrightarrow ext{Plasma Membrane (exocytosis)}$$
2. The Bioenergetic Coupling Pipeline:
$$ ext{Chloroplast (converts solar energy into glucose)} \longleftrightarrow ext{Mitochondria (oxidizes glucose into ATP)} \longrightarrow ext{Powers Active Transport \& Biosynthesis}$$
3. The Cellular Maintenance & Autophagy System:
$$ ext{Damaged Organelles / Pathogens} \longrightarrow ext{Engulfed in Vacuole} \longrightarrow ext{Fuses with Lysosome} \longrightarrow ext{Hydrolyzed into Monomers for Cytoplasmic Reuse}$$

Analysis Questions & Solutions (Page 13)

  1. Explain how at least 3 organelles work together as an integrated organelle system:
    - Answer: In the protein synthesis pathway, the nucleus transcribes the genetic code of DNA into mRNA; ribosomes attached to the rough ER translate mRNA into a polypeptide chain that is folded in the ER lumen; the ER packages the immature protein into a vesicle that delivers it to the Golgi apparatus for final carbohydrate modification and dispatch to the plasma membrane.
  2. Why is the smooth endoplasmic reticulum important to the cell? What does it produce and how are its products used in the cell?
    - Answer: The Smooth ER synthesizes membrane phospholipids and cholesterol required to build and repair all cellular membranes, produces steroid hormones, and detoxifies alcohol, pharmaceutical drugs, and metabolic byproducts in liver hepatocytes.
  3. What would be a consequence of a plant having a dysfunctional chloroplast?
    - Answer: The plant cell could not capture sunlight or synthesize glucose via photosynthesis. Without glucose to fuel mitochondrial respiration, ATP levels collapse, and the plant starves and dies.
  4. A cell is observed under a microscope and contains a large number of mitochondria. What might this suggest about the function of the cell, and why? What would an example be?
    - Answer: It indicates the cell has an extremely high metabolic demand for continuous ATP production. Examples include skeletal muscle cells (for contraction), cardiac myocytes (continuous heart pumping), and sperm cells (powering flagellar swimming).
  5. Prokaryotic cells lack membrane-bound organelles, while eukaryotic cells contain them. How do membrane-bound organelles give eukaryotic cells an advantage in terms of efficiency and complexity?
    - Answer: Compartmentalization. Membrane-bound organelles isolate incompatible chemical reactions (e.g., acidic hydrolytic enzymes inside lysosomes), concentrate reactants and specific enzymes in localized microenvironments, and dramatically increase internal surface area for metabolic processes.

9. Page 15: Introduction to Microscope Use & Slide Lab Questions

Microscope Protocol Fill-in-the-Blank (Page 15)

  1. Place the slide on the microscope stage.
  2. Use stage clips to secure the slide in place.
  3. Always start on the lowest power objective (4X scanning power).
  4. Look into the eyepiece (ocular lens); you should see a pointer (arrow) for referencing specific areas on the slide.
  5. Use the coarse focus knob first; this will be the larger knob.
  6. Use the fine focus knob to make the image clear; this will be the smaller knob.
  7. Click the objective to the next highest power, get the image in focus; you should hear the objective click into place.
  8. When you get to the largest objective (40X), DO NOT use the coarse focusing knob; use the fine focus knob ONLY.

Slide Lab Analysis Questions (Page 15)

  1. Why should you always begin with the lowest magnification?
    - It provides the widest field of view and largest depth of focus, making it easiest to locate the specimen without colliding the lens into the slide.
  2. What is the general shape of a typical plant cell? Animal cell?
    - Plant cells have a rigid, fixed, angular rectangular/box-like shape; animal cells have an irregular, flexible, rounded shape.
  3. Why are the prepared slides you viewed stained a certain color?
    - Most cellular structures and organelles are transparent; stains (like iodine, methylene blue, or crystal violet) bind selectively to specific molecules (nucleic acids, cell walls) to provide optical contrast.
  4. Why must the specimen you are viewing be only 1 cell layer thick?
    - In a compound light microscope, light must be able to pass directly through the specimen to reach the objective lens and ocular. Thick multi-layered specimens block light and appear as black silhouettes.

Station 1: Macromolecules & Organelles (Page 17)

  1. Cell Wall Macromolecule: Carbohydrate (Polysaccharide - Cellulose); provides tensile strength and rigid structural support.
  2. Ribosome Subunits: Composed of Nucleic Acids (rRNA) and Proteins; rRNA catalyzes peptide bond formation during translation.
  3. Cell Membrane Regulation: Lipids (Phospholipids & Cholesterol); nonpolar hydrophobic core creates a selectively permeable barrier.
  4. Cytoskeleton Filaments: Proteins (Polypeptides - Actin & Tubulin); assemble into microfilaments and microtubules for cell movement and support.
  5. Organelle with almost nothing but nucleic acids: Nucleolus (dense cluster of rRNA transcripts and DNA coding for ribosomes).
  6. Organelle specialized in producing membrane lipids: Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum (Smooth ER).

Station 2: Specialized Human Cells (Pages 17–18)

  1. Red Blood Cells (Erythrocytes):
    - Function: Transports $O_2$ from lung alveoli to tissues and returns $CO_2$.
    - Structure-Function Relationship: Biconcave disc shape maximizes surface-area-to-volume ratio for rapid gas exchange and allows flexing through capillaries; ejects nucleus and mitochondria to maximize hemoglobin volume.
  2. Nerve Cells (Neurons):
    - Function: Transmits electrical nerve impulses across the nervous system.
    - Structure-Function Relationship: Long branched axon conducts action potentials over long anatomical distances; dendrites receive synaptic signals.
    - Myelin Sheath Organelle: Smooth ER produces lipids/myelin; if damaged, electrical conduction slows or halts (as seen in Multiple Sclerosis).
  3. Sperm Cells (Spermatozoa):
    - 3 Largest Parts: Head (Nucleus with DNA), Midpiece (packed with Mitochondria), and Flagellum (Microtubule tail).
    - Importance: Head delivers genetic blueprint to egg, mitochondria generate ATP continuously, and flagellum provides swimming propulsion.

Station 3: Organelles and Human Genetic Disorders (Pages 18–19)

  1. Cystic Fibrosis (CF):
    - Defective Structure: Cell Membrane (CFTR Chloride Channel Protein).
    - Determination: Mutation prevents chloride export; without salt movement, water does not follow by osmosis, causing dehydrated, thick sticky lung mucus.
  2. Muscular Dystrophy / Mitochondrial Myopathy:
    - Defective Organelle: Mitochondria.
    - Determination: Muscle and nerve tissues require vast ATP; failure to produce sufficient cellular energy leads to muscle weakness and heart failure.
  3. I-Cell Disease:
    - Defective Organelle: Golgi Apparatus.
    - Determination: Golgi fails to add mannose-6-phosphate sorting tags to hydrolytic enzymes, mistakenly exporting them outside the cell and creating toxic cytoplasmic inclusion bodies.
  4. Tay-Sachs Disease:
    - Defective Organelle: Lysosomes.
    - Determination: Missing hexosaminidase A enzyme prevents degradation of GM2 ganglioside lipids, which accumulate to toxic levels in neuronal lysosomes and destroy brain tissue.
  5. Why can DNA mutations be harmful to cell function?
    - DNA encodes the amino acid sequences of all enzymes and structural proteins. A mutation can alter protein folding, rendering transport channels, enzymes, or structural polymers nonfunctional.

Station 5: Oddballs of the Natural World — Protists (Page 19)

  1. Paramecium:
    - Features: Covered in rhythmic cilia for swimming; oral groove for sweeping in bacteria; contractile vacuoles to expel hypotonic freshwater.
    - Classification: Animal-like protist (Protozoan) because it is heterotrophic and actively motile.
  2. Euglena:
    - Features: Green chloroplasts for photosynthesis; whip-like flagellum; red eyespot (stigma) to detect light direction; contractile vacuole.
    - Classification: Mixotroph (both Plant- and Animal-like) because it performs autotrophic photosynthesis in light and heterotrophic feeding in darkness.
  3. Noctiluca ("Sea Sparkle"):
    - Features: Marine dinoflagellate with bioluminescent luciferin reaction; forms large colonies; can produce toxic red tides.
    - Classification: Plant/Animal-like marine protist that combines photosynthesis with phagotrophic feeding on zooplankton.

Station 6: Organelles that Build Proteins (Pages 19–20)

The 5-Step Protein Synthesis Pipeline

  • Step 1: Proteins are made by Ribosomes (translating mRNA).
  • Step 2: Protein folded on the Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum.
  • Step 3: Transported in a transport vesicle to the Golgi apparatus.
  • Step 4: Processed in the Golgi apparatus for finishing touches, chemical tagging, and packaging.
  • Step 5: Transported by another secretory vesicle to the plasma membrane to be exported via exocytosis.

Analysis Questions (Page 20)

  1. What would happen if the ribosome stopped functioning correctly?
    - Translation halts; no new polypeptides, enzymes, or transport channels can be synthesized, resulting in cell death.
  2. If the cell were missing vesicles, how would this affect protein production?
    - Proteins folded in the Rough ER could never be shuttled to the Golgi apparatus or exported to the cell membrane.
  3. Where are the instructions to making a protein located inside the cell?
    - Inside the nucleus, encoded in the nucleotide sequence of DNA.
  4. What would happen to the protein if there were errors in the cell's DNA?
    - Mutations lead to altered mRNA codons, potentially inserting incorrect amino acids, causing protein misfolding, nonfunctional enzyme active sites, or genetic disease.
