  • Target 1: I can explain how organelles interact as a system to help cells maintain homeostasis.
  • Target 2: I can match each organelle to its correct physical description and function.

🔬 Cell Organelles: Background & Systems Concept

  • Organelles: Small, specialized structures inside a cell that perform specific cellular functions.
  • System Concept: When organelles all work together seamlessly as an integrated system, the cell maintains homeostasis (the stable, balanced internal environment required for survival).
  • Review Question: What is homeostasis?
  • Answer: Homeostasis is the ability of an organism or cell to regulate and maintain a constant, stable internal physiological environment despite fluctuating external conditions.

🧱 Organelle Directory: Structure and Function

1. Cytoplasm

  • Physical Description: A jelly-like, fluid substance (cytosol) filling the interior of the cell.
  • Function: Contains dissolved nutrients, ions, and biomolecules; cushions and suspends all of the cell’s organelles.

2. Cytoskeleton

  • Physical Description: An extensive network of structural proteins (microfilaments, intermediate filaments, and microtubules).
  • Function: Provides structural framework and shape for the cell; enables intracellular transport and anchors organelles in designated positions.

3. Nucleus

  • Physical Description: Enclosed by a double-membrane nuclear envelope perforated with nuclear pores that strictly regulate molecular traffic. Contains chromatin (DNA and proteins) and the nucleolus.
  • Function:
  • Storehouse and guardian for the cell’s genetic material (DNA).
  • Balances two major operational demands:
    1. DNA must be protected from damage within the cytoplasm.
    2. DNA must be accessible for transcription into RNA to direct protein synthesis.

4. Mitochondria

  • Physical Description: Bean-shaped organelle with a smooth outer membrane and an inner membrane folded into extensive ridges called cristae.
  • Contains its own unique circular DNA and independent ribosomes.
  • Evolutionary Note (Endosymbiotic Theory): These traits strongly indicate that mitochondria were once ancient, free-living aerobic prokaryotes engulfed by primitive eukaryotic host cells.
  • Function: The powerhouse of the cell; produces cellular energy in the form of ATP via cellular respiration.

5. Golgi Apparatus

  • Physical Description: Closely layered stacks of membrane-enclosed flattened sacs (cisternae).
  • Function: Modifies, sorts, packages, and tags proteins and lipids received from the ER; dispatches them in membrane-bound vesicles to destinations inside or outside the cell.
  • Vesicles: Small, membrane-bound transport spheres that isolate and move substances throughout the cytoplasm.

6. Ribosomes

  • Physical Description: Non-membrane-bound complexes composed of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and catalytic proteins; consist of a large and small subunit.
  • Function: The site of protein synthesis (translation); reads messenger RNA (mRNA) sequences and links transfer RNA (tRNA)-delivered amino acids into polypeptide chains.

7. Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER)

The ER is an extensive, continuous labyrinth of folded membranes extending from the nuclear envelope:

Feature Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum (Rough ER) Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum (Smooth ER)
Physical Appearance Studded with ribosomes across outer surface; rough/granular texture Free of ribosomes; smooth, tubular membranous network
Primary Functions • Synthesis and processing of proteins destined for membranes or secretion
• Initial protein folding and glycosylation inside lumen		 • Synthesis of essential lipids (phospholipids, steroids)
• Detoxification of drugs, alcohol, and metabolic poisons
• Calcium ion ($Ca^{2+}$) storage

📝 Check for Understanding: Day 1

Day 1 Closer Questions

  1. Which image shows Rough ER? Which image shows Smooth ER?
    - Image with studded dots (ribosomes) represents Rough ER; tubular network without dots represents Smooth ER.
  2. Why do they look different?
    - Rough ER appears bumpy because it is embedded with ribosomes on its cytosolic face for protein synthesis. Smooth ER lacks ribosomes because its specialized enzymatic machinery is dedicated to lipid metabolism and toxin breakdown.

📝 Warm Up: Day 2 — Systems & Organelle Interactions

Statement: Organelles interact as a system within a cell to maintain homeostasis. Each one plays an important role.

  1. Support this statement with 3 examples of organelles. Name each organelle and explain why it is important to the cell:
    - Nucleus: Houses DNA and provides the genetic blueprints for cellular enzymes and structural proteins.
    - Ribosomes: Directly translate RNA messages from the nucleus to synthesize essential functional proteins.
    - Mitochondria: Generate ATP to power active transport, chemical biosynthesis, and cellular movement.
  2. If one organelle fails to perform its role, what would happen to the cell?
    - Because organelles operate as an interconnected system, the failure of a single organelle disrupts cellular homeostasis. For instance, if the mitochondria fail, ATP levels plummet, protein synthesis halts, active transport across the cell membrane collapses, and the cell undergoes apoptosis or necrosis.

🧬 Additional Organelles & Specialized Structures

8. Lysosomes

  • Physical Description: Spherical, single membrane-bound organelles filled with powerful digestive enzymes (hydrolases) that operate at an acidic pH.
  • Function:
  • Serves as the cell’s waste disposal and recycling center: breaks down damaged, worn-out organelles (autophagy).
  • Defends the cell against invading pathogens by fusing with phagocytic vacuoles to destroy engulfed bacteria and viruses.

9. Centrosome (with Centrioles)

  • Physical Description: Specialized non-membrane-bound region located near the nucleus in animal cells; contains a pair of barrel-shaped microtubule structures called centrioles.
  • Function:
  • Coordinates spindle fiber formation to separate chromosomes during cell division (mitosis and meiosis).
  • Organizes the basal bodies that nucleate cilia and flagella.

10. Cell Membrane (Plasma Membrane)

  • Physical Description: Flexible phospholipid bilayer with embedded integral/peripheral proteins and cholesterol.
  • Function:
  • Defines cellular boundary; establishes selective permeability.
  • Regulates the import and export of nutrients, ions, signaling molecules, and wastes to preserve internal equilibrium.

11. Vacuoles

  • Physical Description: Membrane-bound storage sacs.
  • Animal Cells: Multiple small, temporary vacuoles scattered throughout the cytoplasm.
  • Plant Cells: A single, massive central vacuole occupying up to 90% of mature cell volume.
  • Function:
  • Stores water, carbohydrates, mineral salts, and metabolic byproducts.
  • In plant cells, maintains hydrostatic pressure (turgor pressure) against the rigid cell wall, providing structural rigidity to the plant.

12. Cell Wall

  • Physical Description: Rigid outer protective layer located immediately outside the plasma membrane.
  • Found in: Plants & Algae (cellulose), Fungi (chitin), and Bacteria (peptidoglycan). Never found in animal cells.
  • Function: Provides structural support, tensile strength, protection against physical damage, and prevents cell lysis in hypotonic environments.

13. Chloroplasts

  • Physical Description: Double membrane-bound organelle containing thylakoid disks stacked into grana, surrounded by stroma.
  • Found exclusively in plant cells and photosynthetic algae.
  • Contains own circular DNA and 70S ribosomes (evidence of endosymbiosis).
  • Function: Absorbs solar radiant energy using chlorophyll pigments to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose during photosynthesis.
