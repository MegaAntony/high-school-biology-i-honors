Level 1 · Beginner

The big idea

🔁 Metabolism

Metabolism is every chemical reaction in an organism. Catabolism breaks big molecules into smaller ones (and usually releases energy). Anabolism builds big molecules from small ones (and usually needs energy).

Digestion is a catabolic example: food is broken into molecules cells can use.

🧲 Why enzymes?

Reactions need an activation energy, an energy barrier to get started. Without help many would take far too long. A catalyst lowers the barrier so the reaction runs faster.

Enzymes are biological catalysts, mostly proteins. They do not change the total energy of reactants or products.

Key words in one picture

Substrate
The reactant the enzyme acts on.
Active site
The 3D pocket where the substrate binds.
Enzyme-substrate complex
The temporary pairing during the reaction.
Products
What leaves. The enzyme is free to reuse.

Lock and key: only substrates whose shape fits the active site bind. Real enzymes also change shape a little when the substrate binds (induced fit).

⚡ Energy changes

  • Exergonic: products have less free energy than reactants. Energy is released. (Catabolic reactions are usually exergonic.)
  • Endergonic: products have more free energy. Energy must be added. (Anabolic reactions are usually endergonic.)

An enzyme lowers activation energy in either case. It does not decide whether a reaction releases or needs energy.

🔋 ATP in one line

ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the cell's rechargeable energy carrier. Breaking off its third phosphate releases energy to power work.

ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pᵢ + energy

Level 2 · Core

Enzymes: the graphs and rules

Activation energy with and without an enzyme

  • 🟣 Purple curve (no enzyme): high energy barrier, tall Eₐ.
  • 🟢 Green curve (with enzyme): lower barrier, short Eₐ.
  • 🔴 Red arrow (ΔG): the net energy change. It is the same with or without the enzyme.

Trap: the enzyme changes how fast the reaction happens, not how much energy is released or absorbed overall.

🌡️ Temperature

Rate rises as temperature rises (more collisions), up to an optimum near body temperature (about 37–40 °C in the teacher's graph B).

Past the optimum the enzyme denatures: hydrogen and ionic bonds break and the active site loses its shape. Denaturation is usually not reversed, so the enzyme stops working.

🧪 pH

Each enzyme has a pH optimum. Moving far from it changes the bonds that hold the protein's shape, lowering activity or denaturing the enzyme.

EnzymeOptimum pHWhere
Pepsin≈ 1.5–2.0Stomach
Salivary amylase≈ 6.8–7.0Mouth
Trypsin≈ 8.0–8.5Small intestine

Substrate concentration and saturation

  1. a → b (rising): more substrate means more active sites are filled and more collisions. Rate rises quickly.
  2. c (plateau): all active sites are full. The enzyme is saturated and working at its maximum rate (Vmax).
  3. Past c: adding substrate does nothing. Only more enzyme can speed the reaction up.

Allosteric activation works the same way in reverse: an effector binds the allosteric site and makes the active site better at binding substrate. It lets cells switch enzymes on and off.

ATP structure

  • Adenine (base)
  • Ribose (5-carbon sugar)
  • Three phosphate groups (the energy part)

Adenine + ribose = adenosine. "Tri" = three phosphates. The three negative charges repel each other, which makes ATP unstable and ready to release energy.

Hydrolysis, phosphorylation, and the cycle

  • Hydrolysis: water removes the third phosphate. ATP → ADP + Pᵢ, releasing usable energy.
  • Phosphorylation: the phosphate is moved onto another molecule, changing its shape or activity.
  • Recycling: energy from food breakdown adds a phosphate back to ADP to reform ATP.

ATP is a rechargeable carrier, not long-term storage. Fats and glycogen store energy long-term.

Exam quick reference

• Enzymes lower Eₐ. They do not change ΔG, and they are not used up.

• Rate plateaus when enzymes are saturated. Only more enzyme raises the maximum rate.

• Heat or extreme pH denatures the enzyme, so the active site no longer fits the substrate.

• Competitive inhibition can be overcome with more substrate. Noncompetitive inhibition cannot.

• ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pᵢ + energy. ADP is re-phosphorylated using energy from food breakdown.

Level 3 · Expert

Read the graph, predict the answer

Most MCQs give you a graph or a table. Find the variable on each axis, the shape of the curve, and what limits the rate at each point.

1 · Three limiting factors ▼
What limits the rate?Signal on the graphFix
Substrate (too little)Rate rises steeply with more substrateAdd substrate
Enzyme (all active sites full)Flat plateau, adding substrate does nothingAdd more enzyme
Shape loss (denaturation)Rate falls sharply after the optimumNothing: the enzyme is damaged

Trap: a falling rate on a temperature or pH graph means denaturation, not "running out of substrate."

2 · Why more substrate beats competitive inhibition ▼

Both inhibitors compete for or disturb the same enzyme. The question is whether the substrate can out-compete the inhibitor for the active site.

  • Competitive: the inhibitor sits in the active site, so more substrate can push it out (the rate can still reach its maximum).
  • Noncompetitive: the inhibitor changes the enzyme's shape, so the active site is distorted even when substrate is plentiful. The maximum rate drops.

Extension (beyond the teacher's slides): on a substrate-rate graph, competitive inhibition shifts the curve to the right with the same maximum, and noncompetitive inhibition lowers the maximum.

3 · Station 1 answers, explained ▼

Diagram 1 (substrate → products): catabolic. One larger substrate becomes two smaller products. The enzyme is unchanged.

pH graph: pepsin works in acid (stomach), trypsin in base (small intestine). Salivary amylase is near neutral, so at pH 2 its activity is about zero. Its bonds are disrupted and it denatures.

Temperature graph (B): peak near 37–40 °C, then denaturation after the optimum. Once the active site is distorted, the enzyme-substrate complex can't form.

Saturation graph (C): rate rises quickly from a to b, levels off at c because every active site is occupied.

4 · Energy coupling: why cells use ATP ▼

Cells couple an exergonic reaction (ATP hydrolysis, or glucose breakdown) to an endergonic one (building a molecule or moving a substance). The energy released by one drives the other.

Why not release all the energy from glucose at once? The teacher's slide 25 answer: most of it would be lost as heat. Small steps let the cell capture part of it in ATP.

Short-answer practice

Write your answer on paper first, then open the model. Use Claim → Evidence → Reasoning.

Q1 · Explain how an enzyme speeds up a reaction without changing ΔG. (4 pts)

Model: An enzyme provides an active site where the substrate binds, forming an enzyme-substrate complex. Binding strains the substrate's bonds and orients the molecules, so the reaction needs less energy to start (lower activation energy). The enzyme is not consumed and is released unchanged. Because the enzyme only changes the pathway, the free energy difference between reactants and products (ΔG) stays the same.

Scoring: active site binds substrate (1) · lowers activation energy (1) · enzyme is unchanged and reusable (1) · ΔG unchanged (1).

Q2 · Compare competitive and noncompetitive inhibition. Which can more substrate overcome? (4 pts)

Model: A competitive inhibitor binds directly in the active site and blocks the substrate. A noncompetitive inhibitor binds an allosteric site, changes the enzyme's shape, and distorts the active site. Adding more substrate can overcome competitive inhibition because substrate molecules outnumber the inhibitor at the active site. It cannot overcome noncompetitive inhibition, because the shape change is not a competition.

Scoring: competitive binds active site (1) · noncompetitive binds allosteric site and changes shape (1) · more substrate overcomes competitive (1) · not noncompetitive (1).

Q3 · Explain why a pH of 2 would stop salivary amylase from working. (3 pts)

Model: Salivary amylase works best near neutral pH (about 6.8–7.0). At pH 2 the high H⁺ concentration disrupts the ionic and hydrogen bonds that hold its 3D shape. The active site loses its shape, so the substrate can no longer bind, and activity drops to about zero (denaturation).

Scoring: optimum is near neutral (1) · H⁺ disrupts bonds / shape (1) · active site no longer binds substrate (1).

Q4 · Explain why ATP is a good energy carrier but not a long-term energy store. (3 pts)

Model: ATP's phosphate bonds are unstable because the three negative charges repel each other, so hydrolysis releases usable energy quickly. Cells regenerate ATP continuously from ADP using energy from food breakdown, which makes it a rechargeable carrier. Long-term storage needs large, stable molecules like fats or glycogen, not a molecule that is constantly broken down and rebuilt.

Scoring: unstable phosphate bonds release energy (1) · ATP is recycled from ADP (1) · long-term storage needs stable molecules (1).
