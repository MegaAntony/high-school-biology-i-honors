Station 1: Enzymes (Page 1)

1. Enzyme-Substrate Diagram Labels & Reaction Type

  • Top-left box: Substrate (the reactant molecule composed of bonded hexagons 1 and 2).
  • Middle-left box pointing to pocket: Active Site (the specific 3D pocket on the enzyme where the substrate binds).
  • Bottom-left box: Enzyme (remains unchanged before and after the reaction).
  • Bottom-middle box: Enzyme-Substrate Complex (temporary intermediate formed when substrate binds via induced fit).
  • Top-right box: Products (two separate monomers 1 and 2 released after bond cleavage).
  • Bottom-right box: Enzyme (recycled and ready to bind another substrate).
  • Question: Is this enzyme catalyzing a catabolic or anabolic reaction? How do you know?
  • Answer: Catabolic reaction (hydrolysis/breakdown). You can tell because a single, larger bonded substrate molecule enters the active site and is broken apart into two smaller, separate product molecules.

2. Competitive vs. Non-Competitive Inhibition Diagrams

  • Left Diagram (Pink teardrop blocking green circle from entering the active site): Competitive Inhibition.
  • Mechanism: The competitive inhibitor has a shape similar to the natural substrate and binds directly inside the active site, physically blocking the substrate from binding. It can be overcome by adding a high concentration of substrate.
  • Right Diagram (Small red triangle binding to bottom site, warping the top active site): Non-Competitive (Allosteric) Inhibition.
  • Mechanism: The inhibitor binds to a separate allosteric site (away from the active site), causing a conformational (3D shape) change in the enzyme that distorts the active site so the substrate can no longer fit or react properly. It cannot be overcome by adding more substrate.

3. Graph A Questions (pH vs. Enzyme Activity: Pepsin, Salivary Amylase, Trypsin)

  1. Which enzyme works best under acidic conditions?
  2. Pepsin (optimum $\text{pH} \approx 1.5\text{–}2.0$ in the stomach).
  3. Which enzyme works best under alkaline (basic) conditions?
  4. Trypsin (optimum $\text{pH} \approx 8.0\text{–}8.5$ in the small intestine).
  5. Would salivary amylase still be able to function if it was exposed to an environment with a pH of 2? Why or why not?
  6. No. Salivary amylase has an optimal pH around 6.8–7.0 (neutral). At $\text{pH } 2$, the activity curve for salivary amylase is at 0 because the extreme concentration of $\text{H}^+$ ions disrupts the ionic and hydrogen bonds maintaining its 3D tertiary structure, causing the enzyme to denature (lose its active site shape).

4. Graph B Questions (Temperature vs. Specific Activity)

  1. At what temperature does this enzyme work best?
  2. Approximately $37^\circ\text{C}\text{–}40^\circ\text{C}$ (Point B, the peak of the curve).
  3. What process is happening to the enzyme between point B and C?
  4. Denaturation (thermal unfolding of the protein as excessive kinetic energy breaks hydrogen and ionic bonds).
  5. What happens to the active site of an enzyme when the temperature is too high? Would the enzyme still be able to function? Why or why not?
  6. When the temperature is too high, the active site loses its specific 3D shape (unfolds/warps). The enzyme will no longer function because the substrate can no longer fit/bind into the distorted active site to form an enzyme-substrate complex.

5. Graph C Questions (Substrate Concentration vs. Reaction Rate $V$)

  1. What is happening between point a and b?
  2. As substrate concentration increases, the reaction rate increases rapidly because there are plenty of open, available active sites, leading to more frequent collisions between enzymes and substrates.
  3. What is happening at point c?
  4. Point of Saturation ($V_{\max}$): The reaction rate levels off into a flat plateau. Every enzyme active site is continuously occupied by substrate.
  5. Why won't the reaction rate increase past the point of saturation?
  6. Because all enzyme active sites are completely full (saturated) and working at maximum speed. Enzyme concentration is now the limiting factor; adding more substrate cannot speed up the reaction unless more enzyme molecules are added.

Station 2: Energy (Page 2)

1. Free Energy Graphs A and B

  • Graph A ($\Delta G < 0$, Products lower than Reactants):
  • Reaction Type: Exergonic (Catabolic) Reaction (e.g., Cellular Respiration).
  • Left plateau: Reactants (high initial free energy).
  • Peak of hill: Transition State / Activation Energy ($E_a$) barrier.
  • Vertical arrow ($\Delta G < 0$): Net Energy Released.
  • Right plateau: Products (low final free energy).
  • Graph B ($\Delta G > 0$, Products higher than Reactants):
  • Reaction Type: Endergonic (Anabolic) Reaction (e.g., Photosynthesis).
  • Left plateau: Reactants (low initial free energy).
  • Vertical arrow ($\Delta G > 0$): Net Energy Added / Absorbed.
  • Right plateau: Products (high final free energy stored in chemical bonds).

2. Enzyme Activation Energy Graph Labels

  • Left starting line: Reactants.
  • Top purple curve: Reaction without the enzyme.
  • Lower green curve: Reaction with the enzyme.
  • Tall thin vertical double-arrow (from reactants to purple peak): Activation energy ($E_a$) without the enzyme.
  • Short thin vertical double-arrow (from reactants to green peak): Activation energy ($E_a$) with the enzyme.
  • Thick red vertical double-arrow (from reactants level down to products level): Overall energy released ($\Delta G$) — Note: identical with or without the enzyme!
  • Right bottom line: Products.
  • Question: How do enzymes speed up the rate of chemical reactions?
  • Answer: Enzymes speed up chemical reactions by lowering the activation energy ($E_a$) required to start the reaction (by binding substrates in the optimal orientation and straining their chemical bonds), allowing more reactant molecules to reach the transition state per second without changing the overall free energy change ($\Delta G$).

Station 3: Relating Cellular Respiration and Photosynthesis (Page 2)

Feature / Parameter Cellular Respiration Photosynthesis
Role of Oxygen ($\text{O}_2$) Reactant (Input): Final electron acceptor at the end of the ETC; combines with $\text{e}^-$ and $\text{H}^+$ to form $\text{H}_2\text{O}$. Product (Output / Byproduct): Released during the Light-Dependent Reactions when water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) is split (photolysis) at Photosystem II.
Role of $\text{CO}_2$ Product (Output / Waste): Released during Pyruvate Oxidation ($2\text{ CO}_2$) and the Krebs Cycle ($4\text{ CO}_2$) as glucose is oxidized. Reactant (Input): Enters the Stroma and is fixed by the enzyme RuBisCO during the Calvin Cycle to build G3P/glucose.
Role of Glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$) Reactant (Input): High-energy organic fuel broken down (oxidized) beginning in glycolysis to harvest electrons and make ATP. Product (Output): High-energy sugar synthesized from G3P in the Calvin Cycle to store chemical energy and build plant biomass.
Role of Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) Product (Output): Formed at the end of the ETC when $\text{O}_2$ accepts electrons and protons ($2\text{H}^+$). Reactant (Input): Split by light energy (photolysis) at Photosystem II to supply replacement electrons, $\text{H}^+$ ions, and $\text{O}_2$.
Role of ATP Main Product (Output): $36\text{–}38\text{ ATP}$ produced per glucose to power cellular work (2 in Glycolysis, 2 in Krebs, 32–34 in ETC). Intermediate Energy Carrier: Produced in the Light Reactions (thylakoid) and immediately consumed in the Calvin Cycle (stroma) to build glucose.
WHO does it? ALL Eukaryotic Organisms (both Autotrophs/Plants AND Heterotrophs/Animals, Fungi, Protists; prokaryotes do glycolysis/fermentation/respiration in cytosol/membrane). Photoautotrophs (Producers): Plants, algae, and photosynthetic bacteria (cyanobacteria).
WHERE does it occur in the cell? Cytosol/Cytoplasm (Glycolysis) and Mitochondria (Matrix = Pyruvate Oxidation & Krebs; Inner Membrane/Cristae = ETC). Chloroplasts (Thylakoid Membrane = Light-Dependent Reactions; Stroma = Calvin Cycle).
WHY does it occur? To release stored chemical energy from organic food (glucose) and convert it into usable cellular energy (ATP). To capture solar light energy and convert/store it as stable chemical energy in the bonds of organic molecules (glucose).
  • Question: How are cellular respiration and photosynthesis related processes? Include anabolic and catabolic in your explanation.
  • Answer: Photosynthesis and cellular respiration form a complementary biogeochemical cycle of matter and flow of energy. Photosynthesis is an anabolic, endergonic process that absorbs solar energy to build high-energy glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$) and release $\text{O}_2$ from low-energy $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$. Conversely, cellular respiration is a catabolic, exergonic process that breaks down that glucose in the presence of $\text{O}_2$ to release $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ while transferring chemical energy into ATP. The products of photosynthesis ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\text{O}_2$) are the exact reactants of cellular respiration, and the products of cellular respiration ($6\text{CO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}$) are the exact reactants of photosynthesis (while energy flows one-way: $\text{Sunlight} \to \text{Glucose} \to \text{ATP} \to \text{Heat}$).

Station 4 Follow-Up Questions:

  1. What is the process that occurs after glycolysis, when no oxygen is available in animals, and in bacteria?
  2. Lactic Acid Fermentation (converts pyruvate into lactic acid/lactate to oxidize $\text{NADH}$ back to $\text{NAD}^+$ so glycolysis can continue).
  3. What is the stage of cellular respiration in which Acetyl-CoA is broken down to release carbon dioxide, and create NADH and $\text{FADH}_2$?
  4. The Krebs Cycle (or Citric Acid Cycle).
  5. What is the process that releases energy by breaking down food molecules in the presence of oxygen?
  6. Aerobic Cellular Respiration.
  7. Kearns-Sayre Syndrome is a mitochondrial disease in which the part of mitochondria responsible for carrying out oxidative phosphorylation is damaged or does not function properly. What specific consequences does this disease have on the process of cellular respiration?
  8. Answer (CER Model):
    • Claim: Kearns-Sayre Syndrome severely reduces cellular ATP production (from $36\text{–}38\text{ ATP}$ down to only $2\text{–}4\text{ ATP}$ per glucose) and forces cells to rely heavily on anaerobic glycolysis and lactic acid fermentation.
    • Evidence / Mechanism: Oxidative phosphorylation (the Electron Transport Chain and ATP Synthase on the inner mitochondrial membrane) normally generates $32\text{–}34\text{ ATP}$ per glucose and oxidizes $\text{NADH}$ and $\text{FADH}_2$ back into $\text{NAD}^+$ and $\text{FAD}$.
    • Reasoning: When oxidative phosphorylation is damaged, the ETC cannot pump protons ($\text{H}^+$) or drive ATP Synthase, eliminating the cell's primary source of ATP. Furthermore, because $\text{NADH}$ and $\text{FADH}_2$ cannot drop off electrons at the ETC, the mitochondrial supply of $\text{NAD}^+$ and $\text{FAD}$ is depleted, halting Pyruvate Oxidation and the Krebs Cycle. High-energy tissues like skeletal muscle, heart muscle, and nerves suffer severe energy starvation, muscle weakness, and lactic acidosis.

Photosynthesis (Overview) Chloroplast $6\text{ CO}_2 + 6\text{ H}_2\text{O} + \text{Light Energy}$ $1\text{ Glucose } (\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6) + 6\text{ O}_2$
Light-Dependent Reactions (Overview) Thylakoid Membrane $\text{Light Energy} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{ADP} + \text{P}_i + \text{NADP}^+$ $\text{O}_2$, $\text{ATP}$, $\text{NADPH}$

Station 5 Follow-Up Questions:

  1. What is the process called by which plants use the sun's energy to make high-energy sugars?
  2. Photosynthesis.
  3. What is RuBisCO? Why is it important?
  4. RuBisCO (ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase) is the carbon-fixing enzyme in the stroma that catalyzes the first step of the Calvin Cycle (attaching inorganic $\text{CO}_2$ gas from the air onto the 5-carbon sugar RuBP). It is the most abundant and important enzyme on Earth because it converts inorganic carbon from the atmosphere into organic biological molecules ("makes life out of air").
  5. What is an organism called that makes its own food? Do these organisms also contain mitochondria? Why or why not?
  6. An organism that makes its own food is called an autotroph (or producer). YES, plant cells (autotrophs) also contain mitochondria! Chloroplasts make glucose (food), but plant cells cannot use glucose directly for cellular work—they still need mitochondria to break down that glucose via cellular respiration to generate ATP for their own cellular processes (especially at night or in non-photosynthetic roots!).
  7. How would the closing of stomata impact photosynthesis?
  8. Closing the stomata (pores on the leaf epidermis regulated by guard cells, e.g., during hot/dry conditions to prevent water loss via transpiration) blocks $\text{CO}_2$ from entering the leaf and causes $\text{O}_2$ to build up inside. Without $\text{CO}_2$ as a reactant for RuBisCO, the Calvin Cycle slows down or stops, halting glucose production (and causing wasteful photorespiration).

Station 6: The Electron Transport Chain (ETC) (Page 4)

1. Diagram Labels (Top = Photosynthesis Thylakoid Membrane; Bottom = Cellular Respiration Inner Mitochondrial Membrane)

  • Top Diagram (Photosynthesis ETC in Thylakoid Membrane):
  • Left & middle lightning bolts: Light energy (photons).
  • Green complex on left ($\text{P680}$): Photosystem II (PSII).
  • Water splitting at bottom left: $\text{H}_2\text{O} \to \frac{1}{2}\text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}^+ + 2\text{e}^-$.
  • Green complex in middle ($\text{P700}$): Photosystem I (PSI).
  • Right-hand carrier reducing $\text{NADP}^+$: $\text{NADP}^+ + \text{H}^+ + 2\text{e}^- \to \mathbf{\text{NADPH}}$ (in Stroma).
  • Purple turbine on right: ATP Synthase (protons $\text{H}^+$ diffuse out from thylakoid lumen into stroma, converting $\mathbf{\text{ADP} + \text{P}_i \to \text{ATP}}$).
  • Bottom Diagram (Cellular Respiration ETC in Cristae / Inner Mitochondrial Membrane):
  • Left box donating electrons: $\text{NADH}$ $\to \text{NAD}^+$.
  • Second box donating electrons: $\text{FADH}_2$ $\to \text{FAD}$.
  • Final electron acceptor at end of chain: $\text{O}_2$ $\to$ combines with $\text{e}^-$ and $\text{H}^+$ to form $\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
  • Right-hand turbine: ATP Synthase (protons $\text{H}^+$ diffuse from high $[\text{H}^+]$ in intermembrane space back into the matrix, converting $\mathbf{\text{ADP} + \text{P}_i \to \text{ATP}}$).

2. ETC Comparison Chart: Cellular Respiration vs. Photosynthesis

Parameter Cellular Respiration ETC Photosynthesis ETC
What is the purpose of the ETC? Use high-energy electrons from food to pump $\text{H}^+$ across the inner mitochondrial membrane, driving ATP Synthase to make $32\text{–}34\text{ ATP}$ for the whole cell. Use light-excited electrons to pump $\text{H}^+$ into the thylakoid lumen to make ATP (ETC 1) and reduce $\text{NADP}^+$ into NADPH (ETC 2) specifically to power the Calvin Cycle.
Who are the electron carriers? $\text{NADH}$ and $\text{FADH}_2$ (deliver electrons to the ETC). $\text{NADP}^+$ / $\text{NADPH}$ (accepts electrons at the end of ETC 2 to carry them to the Calvin Cycle). (Remember the "P" in $\text{NAD\mathbf{P}H}$ stands for Photosynthesis!)
Where is the ETC located? Inner Mitochondrial Membrane (Cristae) ($\text{H}^+$ pumped from Matrix $\to$ Intermembrane Space; diffuses back into Matrix). Thylakoid Membrane inside the Chloroplast ($\text{H}^+$ pumped from Stroma $\to$ Thylakoid Lumen; diffuses back into Stroma).
What supplies the energy for the ETC? Chemical energy from food (Glucose) via high-energy electrons stripped during Glycolysis and the Krebs Cycle. Light energy (Sunlight photons) absorbed by chlorophyll in Photosystems II and I (with replacement electrons from splitting $\text{H}_2\text{O}$).

Station 7: Cellular Respiration Practice Questions (Pages 4–5)

  1. In most hypotheses of early Earth... absence of significant amounts of oxygen gas. What was likely the first metabolic activity to harness ATP to evolve in primitive cells?
  2. Answer: c. Glycolysis, as this does not require organelles nor oxygen
  3. Explanation: Glycolysis takes place in the cytosol (requires no membrane-bound organelles) and is completely anaerobic (requires no $\text{O}_2$), matching early prokaryotic life on an anoxic Earth.
  4. In which type of respiration are alcohol and lactic acid produced?
  5. Answer: b. Anaerobic respiration (Fermentation)
  6. Explanation: Alcoholic and lactic acid fermentation occur in the absence of oxygen to regenerate $\text{NAD}^+$.
  7. Which type of respiration is more efficient and why?
  8. Answer: c. Aerobic respiration because it produces 36 ATP
  9. Explanation: Complete oxidation of glucose with oxygen yields $36\text{–}38\text{ ATP}$ per glucose, whereas anaerobic fermentation yields only $2\text{ ATP}$ (from glycolysis).
  10. Which of the following processes generates the most ATP?
  11. Answer: a. Electron transport chain
  12. Explanation: The ETC + chemiosmosis (oxidative phosphorylation) produces $32\text{–}34\text{ ATP}$, compared to $2\text{ ATP}$ in glycolysis, $2\text{ ATP}$ in Krebs, and $0\text{ ATP}$ in fermentation.
  13. If there is no oxygen available after glycolysis, what is the next step in the respiration pathway?
  14. Answer: c. Fermentation
  15. Explanation: Without $\text{O}_2$, pyruvate cannot enter the mitochondria for aerobic respiration and instead undergoes fermentation in the cytoplasm to recycle $\text{NADH} \to \text{NAD}^+$.
  16. _ is diffused into the inner membrane space during the electron transport chain. _____ is formed as a byproduct when oxygen is reduced in the ETC.
  17. Answer: Note on handout choices: $\text{H}^+$ (protons) are pumped into the intermembrane space, $\text{O}_2$ diffuses into the mitochondria to act as the final electron acceptor, and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is formed as a byproduct when $\text{O}_2$ is reduced (d. O2; H2O is the intended multiple-choice key among the listed options a. CO2; H2O, b. O2; CO2, c. H2O; O2, d. O2; H2O).
  18. All of the following occur during the Krebs cycle EXCEPT:
  19. Statements:
    • I. Acetyl-CoA combines with oxaloacetate to form citrate. (True — occurs in Krebs)
    • II. $\text{FADH}_2$ accepts two electrons to form $\text{FAD}$. (False — $\text{FAD}$ accepts electrons to form $\text{FADH}_2$!)
    • III. $\text{NAD}^+$ is reduced to form $\text{NADH}$. (True — occurs in Krebs)
    • IV. Pyruvate is converted into acetyl-CoA. (False — this is Pyruvate Oxidation / Link Reaction BEFORE the Krebs cycle)
    • V. Carbon dioxide, $\text{NADH}$, and $\text{FADH}_2$, and $\text{ATP}$ are reactants. (False — they are PRODUCTS of the Krebs cycle!)
  20. Answer: b. II, IV, and V
  21. Imagine there are two different cells, cell A and cell B. Explain why lactic acid, carbon dioxide and only 2 ATP are produced in cell A and carbon dioxide, water and up to 32 ATP are produced in cell B.
  22. Answer: a. cell A is an animal cell in an anaerobic environment and cell B is an animal cell in an aerobic environment
  23. Explanation: Only animal/bacterial cells make lactic acid (yeast make ethanol), and only 2 ATP are made in anaerobic conditions vs. 32–38 ATP + water in aerobic conditions.
  24. Heterotrophs take in mass in the form of food... If used for cellular respiration, this mass will eventually be converted into carbon dioxide, _, and ___.
  25. Answer: c. Water; ATP
  26. Explanation: The products of cellular respiration are $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (water), and energy ($\text{ATP}$).
  27. The purpose of a biology lab was to measure the oxygen consumption during seed germination... Considering the chemical reaction for cellular respiration, what must be included in the respirometer containing the germinating seeds?
    • Answer: c. Oxygen (and b. Water to germinate) — Specifically from the chemical equation for cellular respiration ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\text{O}_2 \to 6\text{CO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}$), the seeds already contain stored glucose/starch, so c. Oxygen is the required gas reactant consumed during aerobic cellular respiration.

Station 8: Photosynthesis Practice Questions (Pages 6–7)

  1. Which is not a way in which life is dependent on photosynthesis?
  2. Answer: d. Converting oxygen and hydrogen into water
  3. Explanation: Converting oxygen and hydrogen into water occurs at the end of the Cellular Respiration ETC, NOT photosynthesis (photosynthesis splits water into oxygen and hydrogen!).
  4. Which pigment molecule absorbs blue and red light to provide the energy needed for photosynthesis?
  5. Answer: b. Chlorophyll
  6. Which energy carrying molecule is made during the light reactions to be used during the Calvin cycle?
  7. Answer: d. ATP (Note: b. NADH and c. FADH2 are cellular respiration carriers; photosynthesis uses $\text{ATP}$ and $\text{NADPH}$!).
  8. Which structures facilitate the movement of gases in and out of plant cells/leaves?
  9. Answer: c. Stomata
  10. A student is setting up an experiment looking at the rate of photosynthesis in pea plants. Which of the following factors would NOT affect the student's experiment?
  11. Answer: c. Oxygen concentration
  12. Explanation: Light intensity, temperature (enzyme rate), and $\text{CO}_2$ concentration are all direct inputs/factors driving photosynthesis; $\text{O}_2$ is a released waste product.
  13. Four identical plants are grown under different colored light bulbs. Under which color will the release of oxygen be the slowest?

  1. What are the three parts of ATP and where is the majority of energy stored in ATP?

  3. Where energy is stored: In the high-energy chemical bond between the 2nd and 3rd (terminal) phosphate groups.
  4. What is an autotroph? What is a heterotroph?
  5. Autotroph (Producer): An organism that synthesizes its own organic food molecules from inorganic substances using light (photoautotroph) or chemical energy.
  6. Heterotroph (Consumer): An organism that must consume other organisms or organic matter to obtain energy and carbon.
  7. Which organisms go through cellular respiration? Photosynthesis?
  8. Cellular Respiration: ALL eukaryotic organisms (both autotrophs/plants AND heterotrophs/animals, fungi, protists).
  9. Photosynthesis: Only photoautotrophs (plants, algae, cyanobacteria).
  10. Why does fermentation happen? Where does it happen?
  11. Why: When oxygen ($\text{O}_2$) is absent, the ETC stops. Fermentation happens strictly to oxidize $\text{NADH}$ back into $\text{NAD}^+$ so that glycolysis can continue producing $2\text{ net ATP}$.
  12. Where: In the cytosol (cytoplasm).
  13. Compare and contrast both types of fermentation.
  14. Similarities: Both are anaerobic, occur in the cytosol after glycolysis, use pyruvate and $\text{NADH}$, regenerate $\text{NAD}^+$, and yield $0\text{ additional ATP}$ ($2\text{ net ATP}$ total from glycolysis).
  15. Differences:
    • Lactic Acid Fermentation: Occurs in animal muscle cells and certain bacteria; converts pyruvate directly into lactic acid (lactate) with no $\text{CO}_2$ released; reversible (the human liver converts lactate back into pyruvate once oxygen returns).
    • Alcoholic Fermentation: Occurs in yeast and some microorganisms; converts pyruvate into ethanol (ethyl alcohol) and $\text{CO}_2$ gas; irreversible because $\text{CO}_2$ gas is lost.
  16. What does fermentation recycle? Where does this go and why is it significant?
  17. Fermentation recycles $\text{NAD}^+$ (from $\text{NADH}$). The regenerated $\text{NAD}^+$ goes right back to Glycolysis, where it is required as an electron acceptor to keep splitting glucose and generating $2\text{ ATP}$ to keep the cell alive without oxygen.
  18. Lactic acid fermentation is reversible but alcoholic fermentation is not. Why?
  19. In lactic acid fermentation, all 3 carbons of pyruvate are retained in lactate, so liver enzymes can oxidize lactate back into pyruvate when $\text{O}_2$ becomes available. In alcoholic fermentation, a carbon atom is cleaved off and diffuses away as $\text{CO}_2$ gas (decarboxylation), making the conversion to 2-carbon ethanol permanent/irreversible.
  20. What is the SPECIFIC product of the Calvin Cycle and what is it used to make?
  21. The direct 3-carbon sugar product of the Calvin Cycle is G3P (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate). Two G3P molecules are combined to make glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$), as well as starch, cellulose, lipids, and amino acids. (Most G3P—10 out of every 12—is recycled using ATP to regenerate RuBP).
  22. What is cycled back into the Krebs cycle?
  23. Within the cycle itself, the 4-carbon molecule oxaloacetate is regenerated at the end of each turn to bind the next Acetyl-CoA. Between the Krebs cycle and the ETC, $\text{NAD}^+$, $\text{FAD}$, and $\text{ADP} + \text{P}_i$ are cycled back from the ETC into the matrix so the Krebs cycle can continue.
  24. What is cycled back into the Calvin cycle?
    • Within the Calvin cycle itself, the 5-carbon sugar RuBP (ribulose bisphosphate) is regenerated from G3P using ATP. Meanwhile, oxidized $\text{NADP}^+$ and $\text{ADP} + \text{P}_i$ are cycled back from the Calvin cycle (stroma) to the Light-Dependent Reactions (thylakoid), which recharge and return $\text{ATP}$ and $\text{NADPH}$ to the Calvin cycle.

(Directly from Section IX of Dr. Irwin's Honors_Biology_Cellular_Respiration_Outline.pdf) - Carbon Flow: $\text{Glucose (6C)} \xrightarrow{\text{Glycolysis}} 2\text{ Pyruvate (3C)} \xrightarrow{\text{Pyruvate Oxidation}} 2\text{ Acetyl-CoA (2C)} + 2\text{ CO}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Krebs Cycle}} 4\text{ CO}_2$ ($6\text{ CO}_2$ total exhaled as waste). - Electron Flow: $\text{Glucose bonds} \longrightarrow \text{NADH (10) \& FADH}_2\text{ (2)} \longrightarrow \text{Electron Transport Chain (Cristae)} \longrightarrow \text{Oxygen (O}_2\text{, final electron acceptor)} \longrightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}$. - Energy Flow: $\text{Chemical bonds in Glucose} \longrightarrow \text{High-energy electrons in NADH/FADH}_2 \longrightarrow \text{Electrochemical H}^+\text{ proton gradient in Intermembrane Space} \longrightarrow \text{Mechanical rotation of ATP Synthase} \longrightarrow \text{Chemical bonds of ATP (36–38 ATP)}$.

SA Blueprint 2: Substrate-Level vs. Oxidative Phosphorylation & Role of Oxygen

  • Substrate-Level Phosphorylation: An enzyme directly transfers a phosphate group from a substrate molecule onto $\text{ADP}$ to form $\text{ATP}$. Occurs in Glycolysis ($2\text{ net ATP}$) and the Krebs Cycle ($2\text{ ATP}$) $\longrightarrow$ produces only $4\text{ ATP}$ total per glucose.
  • Oxidative Phosphorylation: Electrons from $10\text{ NADH}$ and $2\text{ FADH}_2$ pass down the ETC, pumping $\text{H}^+$ from the matrix into the intermembrane space. The resulting proton gradient drives $\text{H}^+$ back through ATP Synthase (chemiosmosis), producing $32\text{–}34\text{ ATP}$ per glucose.
  • What happens when Oxygen ($\text{O}_2$) is unavailable?: Without $\text{O}_2$ as the final electron acceptor, the ETC backs up and stops. Because $\text{NADH}$ and $\text{FADH}_2$ can no longer drop off electrons at the ETC, $\text{NAD}^+$ and $\text{FAD}$ are not regenerated, causing Pyruvate Oxidation and the Krebs Cycle to stop as well. The cell must switch to Fermentation in the cytosol purely to regenerate $\text{NAD}^+$ so Glycolysis can keep making $2\text{ net ATP}$.

SA Blueprint 3: Photosynthesis — Connecting the Light Reactions and Calvin Cycle

  • Step 1 (PSII & ETC 1 in Thylakoid Membrane): Light excites electrons in chlorophyll ($\text{P680}$). Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) is split (photolysis) to replace those electrons, releasing $\text{O}_2$ gas and $\text{H}^+$. As electrons move through ETC 1, $\text{H}^+$ is pumped from the stroma into the thylakoid lumen. $\text{H}^+$ diffuses back out into the stroma through ATP Synthase to make ATP.
  • Step 2 (PSI & ETC 2 in Thylakoid Membrane): Light re-energizes electrons at PSI ($\text{P700}$). Electrons pass down ETC 2 to the enzyme $\text{NADP}^+$ reductase, which reduces $\text{NADP}^+ + \text{H}^+$ into NADPH in the stroma.
  • Step 3 (Calvin Cycle in Stroma):
  • Carbon Fixation: The enzyme RuBisCO attaches $\text{CO}_2$ onto the 5-carbon sugar RuBP (forming 3-PGA).
  • Reduction: ATP (energy) and NADPH (electrons/$\text{H}^+$) from the light reactions reduce 3-PGA into G3P ($2\text{ G3P}$ exit to form $1\text{ Glucose}$ after 6 turns, using $18\text{ ATP}$ and $12\text{ NADPH}$).
  • Regeneration: The remaining $10\text{ G3P}$ use ATP to regenerate RuBP so the cycle can continue, while $\text{ADP} + \text{P}_i$ and $\text{NADP}^+$ return to the thylakoids.
