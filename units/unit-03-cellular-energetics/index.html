Enzyme Biochemistry & Activation Energy

An enzyme is a biological protein catalyst. It accelerates chemical reactions without being permanently altered or consumed in the process.

⚡ The Activation Energy ($E_a$) Principle

Every chemical reaction has an energetic barrier called the Activation Energy ($E_a$)—the initial energy required to break chemical bonds in the reactants so that new bonds can form.

What Enzymes DO:
• Dramatically lower $E_a$, speeding up reactions millions of times.
• Stabilize the transition state complex.
• Remain reusable and unchanged after releasing products.
What Enzymes NEVER DO:
• Do NOT change $\Delta G$ (the net free energy change of the reaction).
• Do NOT make an endergonic reaction exergonic.
• Do NOT get used up or consumed as a reactant.

Substrate Binding: Lock-and-Key vs. Induced Fit

Lock and Key Model (Fischer, 1894)

Views the enzyme's active site as a rigid, pre-existing geometrical slot where only a substrate with the exact complementary shape fits (like a key into a deadbolt).

Historical model; does not explain transitional bond-bending.
Induced Fit Model (Koshland, 1958 — Accepted)

The active site is flexible. When the substrate enters, chemical interactions cause the enzyme to slightly alter its shape, wrapping snugly around the substrate (like a handshake or a glove).

This physical strain stretches and destabilizes chemical bonds, drastically lowering $E_a$!

Interpreting Environmental Rate Graphs

Dr. Irwin's quizzes always include graphs. Understand the molecular physics behind each curve:

Graph 1: Temperature vs. Reaction Velocity

Asymmetrical Bell Curve
Low Temp (0°C – 20°C): Molecules have low kinetic energy. They move slowly, causing fewer collisions between substrate and active site. Note: Cold does NOT denature!
Optimum Temp (~37°C in humans): Maximum rate of productive collisions per second before structural hydrogen bonds are disrupted.
High Temp (> 45°C - 60°C): Excess thermal motion vibrates and breaks hydrogen and ionic bonds. The enzyme denatures (unfolds irreversibly).

Graph 2: pH vs. Reaction Velocity

Symmetrical Bell Curve
Pepsin (Stomach): Optimum **pH 1.5 – 2.0**. Highly acidic. Denatures quickly if moved to neutral mouth saliva.
Salivary Amylase (Mouth): Optimum **pH 6.8 – 7.0**. Neutral. Digestion starts here, stops in stomach acid.
Trypsin (Small Intestine): Optimum **pH 8.0**. Alkaline due to bicarbonate secretion from the pancreas.

*Molecular cause:* Excess H⁺ (acid) or OH⁻ (base) alters charges on amino acid R-groups, disrupting ionic bonds and denaturing the active site.

Graph 3: Substrate Concentration vs. Reaction Velocity

Hyperbolic Saturation Curve (Plateau)

• Initial Rise: At low substrate levels, active sites are empty. Adding substrate increases collisions and boosts reaction rate linearly.

• Plateau ($V_{\max}$ / Saturation): Every active site is constantly filled. The enzyme is working at maximum catalytic capacity. Enzyme concentration is now the limiting factor. Adding more substrate will NOT speed up the rate.

Enzyme Inhibition: Competitive vs. Allosteric

Inhibitors reduce or eliminate enzyme catalytic activity. Dr. Irwin loves testing the difference between competitive and allosteric inhibitors on rate graphs!

Competitive Inhibitor Blocks Active Site
  • Where it binds: Directly inside the Active Site.
  • Molecular shape: Structurally resembles the real substrate.
  • Can you overcome it? YES! Flooding the test tube with a massive excess of natural substrate out-competes the inhibitor for open active sites.
  • Effect on $V_{\max}$ Graph: $V_{\max}$ is eventually reached, but requires much higher substrate concentrations.
Noncompetitive (Allosteric) Alters Active Site Shape
  • Where it binds: To an Allosteric Site (a regulatory site away from the active site).
  • Molecular mechanism: Causes a conformational change that warps the active site shape so the substrate can no longer fit.
  • Can you overcome it? NO! Adding more substrate has no effect because the active site is distorted.
  • Effect on $V_{\max}$ Graph: $V_{\max}$ is permanently lowered.

The ATP/ADP Energy Cycle

Cells cannot spend raw glucose directly. They convert glucose energy into Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP), the universal energy coin of life.

Anatomy of ATP (3 Key Parts)

1. Nitrogenous Base
Adenine
2. 5-Carbon Pentose
Ribose Sugar
3. Energy Carrier
3 Phosphate Groups
The Hydrolysis & Recharge Reaction:
Energy Release (Hydrolysis / Exergonic):
$$\text{ATP} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \to \text{ADP} + \text{P}_i + \text{Energy}\quad (\Delta G = -7.3\text{ kcal/mol})$$ Breaking the high-energy bond between the 2nd and 3rd phosphate releases energy to power muscle contraction, active transport (Na⁺/K⁺ pump), and protein synthesis.
Recharge (Cellular Respiration / Endergonic):
$$\text{ADP} + \text{P}_i + \text{Energy from Food} \to \text{ATP} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$ The mitochondria continuously adds the 3rd phosphate back onto ADP using energy harvested from glucose breakdown.
05

Photosynthesis vs. Cellular Respiration Preview

Photosynthesis (Chloroplast)
6CO₂ + 6H₂O + Light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

• Type: Endergonic (stores light energy as chemical bonds in glucose).
• Pigments: Chlorophyll a/b in thylakoid membranes absorbing blue/red light (reflecting green).

Cellular Respiration (Mitochondria)
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + 36-38 ATP

• Type: Exergonic (breaks glucose bonds to synthesize ATP).
• Carried out by: Both autotrophs and heterotrophs!
